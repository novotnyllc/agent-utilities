# Printed-mesh garments

Print onto mesh, then attach the mesh to the garment with thread. Test the real
stitch on the real materials before planning anything else, and budget sewing
time at several times a normal-fabric estimate.

## Materials

- **Print on mesh or tulle, never on solid fabric.** PETG flows through the open
  holes and locks round the threads. Pause mid-print, lay the mesh flat (not
  stretched), hold it with magnets or Kapton tape, resume. On lycra/spandex the
  plastic only sits on top and peels off.
- **Foil/metallic spandex takes thread only.** Glue does not grip the slick foil
  (E6000 Plus failed a pull test). Hot glue and UV resin stick, but pull the foil
  off its knit. Foil can't be ironed, so fusibles are out.
- **Plain (non-foil) lycra** can take a stretch fusible (e.g. HeatnBond Soft
  Stretch) to baste the mesh before stitching. Test on a scrap first.

## Test first

Before committing to a plan, sew a scrap of printed mesh onto a scrap of the
actual garment fabric with the exact stitch, needle, thread and settings. Stretch
it hard 10 times. If anything skips, knots, perforates or pops, change the method
now. Discovering a stitch failure late forces method switches that cost hours.

## Machine settings (domestic machine)

What worked on foil spandex (Singer Heavy Duty 6800C class machine):

| Setting | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Stitch | Plain zigzag, width 2.0, length 2.0–2.5 |
| Bar tacks through folded layers | Zigzag width 4.0, length 2.0–2.5, 4 passes (forward, reverse, forward, reverse); a satin length (0.4–0.8) did not feed |
| Tension | 4 |
| Presser foot pressure | Highest the dial allows (a lower setting jammed) |
| Foot | All-purpose, blue painter's tape on the sole, trimmed clear of the needle hole |
| Needle | Super Stretch 75/11 |
| Thread | Polyester |

- **Do not use the triple straight-stretch stitch on foil.** Its forward-back
  feed just stretches the foil back and forth; it sews in one spot, knots and
  perforates. Lower foot pressure, paper above or below, tape on the foot and
  maximum length did not fix it. A walking foot is not made for reverse-feeding
  stitches either.
- A combo serger/coverstitch: coverstitch is ideal for stretch and can sew a mesh
  edge anywhere on a flat piece. Serger mode only works at raw edges.

## Attach: appliqué zigzag on the mesh edge

1. Centre the zigzag on the mesh edge: one swing through mesh and fabric, the
   other onto bare fabric just past the mesh. The thread wraps the edge, so only
   a sliver of bare mesh is needed.
2. Never let a swing land in air past the garment edge. At necklines and armholes
   move the line in until both swings land on garment; trim excess mesh after.
3. Pin every 2 in (5 cm), ¼ in (6 mm) in from the mesh edge, through mesh and
   fabric, never through plastic. Pin on the dress form, then roll the garment off
   from the bottom.
4. Mark every embedded wire crossing (e.g. an LED string) with a tape flag. Stop
   ½ in (13 mm) either side of each one.
5. Stretch the base fabric, not the mesh, gently in front of and behind the foot so
   the mesh lies flat. Let the machine feed; don't pull.
6. Tie off at every start and stop.

## Garments that are already made

- **Open one side seam** with a seam ripper so the garment lies flat. Attach the
  panel flat (coverstitch or appliqué zigzag), then serge the side seam closed,
  catching any mesh edges that run into it. About 10 minutes of ripping and
  re-serging saves hours of wrestling a tube.
- Edges that run around the body (horizontal) feed naturally on a free arm
  without opening a seam.
- Edges that run along the body (side seams) point the wrong way on a free arm.
  Slide the arm in through the neck or the opposite armhole so the body lies
  crosswise and the seam runs over the arm.
- Where front and back panels meet at a side seam, one zigzag straddling both
  mesh edges catches both.

## Design panels for attachment

- Leave ⅜–½ in (10–13 mm) of bare mesh round every edge to be stitched. ¼ in
  (6 mm) proved too tight.
- Stop panels about ¾ in (19 mm) short of armholes, necklines and side seams, and
  make the gap look intentional:
  - fade bubbles smaller and sparser toward the edge into a scatter of tiny
    "fizz" dots;
  - print the outer ½ in as very low 1–2-layer dots the foot can ride over;
  - cut a cloud edge that follows the outer shapes;
  - frame with a trim (ribbon, foil binding).
- Strongest combination: fade plus flat fizz dots at the edge.
- Match mesh colour to the garment. Grey mesh on silver nearly disappears.

## Hand sewing (last resort)

Hand-tacking whole edges is far too slow: over an hour for half a shoulder, and
very hard with poor eyesight. Use it only for short stretches the machine can't
reach.

- Overcast over the mesh edge, a wrap stitch every ½ in (13 mm). Pinch a small
  fold so the needle never touches the form; on a tight garment on a form the
  needle otherwise goes through the whole layer.
- Use a fine needle (sharps 7–9) with a threader. Big needles leave visible holes
  in foil.
- Knots pull through mesh holes. Anchor with a loop-lock through the fabric.

## Related: filling and capping clear ornament balls

- Iridescent shred through ~8 mm (5/16 in) necks: cut it to ½–1 in bits and feed
  through a paper funnel with a toothpick or skewer (a chopstick is too thick), or
  twist 3–4 strands into a rope and push it in. A few wisps is enough to read
  pearly.
- Glue caps with a rice-grain dot of low-temp hot glue on the side of the neck.
  Avoid super glue: its fumes haze clear and iridescent plastic.
